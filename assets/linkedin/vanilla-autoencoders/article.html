Representation learning / episode 01

Demystifying vanilla autoencoders.

An autoencoder does not memorize a picture by magic. It discovers a compact representation that retains the structure needed to draw the picture back.

Give a model 784 input values, compress them to two, then require a reconstruction. The forced loss of information makes the remaining structure visible.

01 — The bottleneck

Compression is the question.

A handwritten 28 × 28 digit occupies 784 coordinates in pixel space. Most possible points in that space are visual noise. Real digits, however, occupy a much smaller and more structured region. The bottleneck asks the model to find that structure rather than reproduce every coordinate independently.

Useful constraint: the encoder has nowhere to hide. If the representation is tiny, it must retain the features that make a digit a digit.

02 — What it learns

An encoder and decoder make a map.

The encoder translates an input into a latent code. The decoder tries to reconstruct the original from that code. Training reduces reconstruction error, but the more interesting result is the geometry: neighboring points in the latent space tend to share visual properties.

ENCODER

Distill

Compresses a high-dimensional observation into a smaller representation.

LATENT

Organize

Places similar structures near each other when the task supports it.

DECODER

Rebuild

Tests whether the latent code preserved what matters.

LOSS

Guide

Rewards reconstructions that stay faithful to the original signal.

03 — What it misses

A clean reconstruction is not a guarantee.

Vanilla autoencoders optimize a reconstruction target, not necessarily useful disentanglement or generative realism. A model can reconstruct common patterns well while smoothing rare details or encoding factors in ways humans cannot interpret. The architecture is a foundation, not a universal representation-learning solution.

04 — In practice

Start with the simplest experiment.

Use a small dataset and a visible bottleneck. Plot the latent coordinates, inspect reconstructions, and change one constraint at a time. That is often a more durable way to understand representation learning than beginning with a much larger model.
